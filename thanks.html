Part I

To scientific inquiry, and to all those who used it

Without the scientific method, and the people who practised it, I would not have known this universe, myself and God, and the relationship between us. These are the minds whose work the two projects use directly, each with what we owe to them.

The field: electricity, magnetism and light9 minds
Charles-Augustin de Coulomb1736–1806

Measured the force between electric charges with his torsion balance (1785): the inverse-square law of step 1 in Maxwell in 3D.

Hans Christian Ørsted1777–1851

Saw a compass needle turn near an electric current (1820): the first link between electricity and magnetism.

André-Marie Ampère1775–1836

The law relating currents to the magnetic field they create (1820s): step 4.

Carl Friedrich Gauss1777–1855

The flux law and the divergence theorem: the two Gauss’s laws of steps 3 and 5.

Michael Faraday1791–1867

Electromagnetic induction (1831), field lines, dielectrics and the ice-pail experiment: the picture of the field behind most of Maxwell in 3D.

James Clerk Maxwell1831–1879

The equations of the electromagnetic field (1865), the displacement current, and the discovery that light is an electromagnetic wave: the whole of Maxwell in 3D, and the light simulated in the Double Slit Lab.

John Henry Poynting1852–1914

Showed where electromagnetic energy flows, S = E × H (1884): step 11.

Heinrich Hertz1857–1894

Produced and detected Maxwell’s waves with a dipole (1887): the radiating antenna of step 13.

Hendrik Lorentz1853–1928

The force on a moving charge, F = q(E + v × B): step 12.

Light and matter as waves and quanta9 minds
Thomas Young1773–1829

The interference of light (1801–1803) and the two-slit experiment itself.

Albert Einstein1879–1955

Light quanta (1905), the photon: why the stripes of light build up one dot at a time.

Louis de Broglie1892–1987

Matter waves, λ = h/p (1924): electrons, neutrons and molecules interfere too; and the first pilot-wave idea (1927).

Erwin Schrödinger1887–1961

The wave equation of quantum mechanics (1926), solved step by step in the Double Slit Lab; and the cat that gave its film a name (1935).

Max Born1882–1970

The rule that |ψ|² gives the probability of finding the particle (1926): where each dot lands.

Werner Heisenberg1901–1976

Matrix mechanics (1925) and the uncertainty principle (1927), measured at the slit in the lab.

Niels Bohr1885–1962

Complementarity (1927), and the great debate with Einstein over the double slit.

David Bohm1917–1992

The pilot-wave theory (1952): the trajectories drawn in the Bohm experiment.

Richard Feynman1918–1988

The Feynman Lectures, where the double slit holds “the only mystery” of quantum mechanics.

Measurement, experiment and quantum optics9 minds
Roy J. Glauber1925–2018

The quantum theory of optical coherence and photodetection (1963), a foundation of quantum optics and of how the lab turns a light wave into single clicks.

Claus Jönsson1930–2024

The first double-slit experiment with electrons (1961).

Akira Tonomura1942–2012

The single-electron build-up of the interference pattern, dot by dot (1989).

Alain Aspect1947–

With Grangier and Roger, proved that true single photons interfere (1986).

Anton Zeilinger1945–

Double-slit diffraction of neutrons (1988) and interference of C₆₀ molecules (1999).

Daniel GreenbergerCity College of New York

With Allaine Yasin, the first quantitative trade-off between path knowledge and fringe visibility (1988).

Berthold-Georg EnglertCentre for Quantum Technologies, Singapore

The duality relation V² + D² ≤ 1 for which-way detectors (1996), the heart of the Which-way experiments.

Marlan Scully1939–

The quantum eraser and which-way detectors without a momentum kick (1982, 1991).

M. Suhail ZubairyTexas A&M University

Quantum optics, quantum information and entanglement; with Marlan Scully, author of the textbook Quantum Optics (1997) and co-author of work on which-way detectors and the quantum eraser.
